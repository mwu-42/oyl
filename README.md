# Oyl

**Own your life.** That's what Oyl stands for: a macOS notch app, and Oyl, the little imp who lives in it, keeping an eye on everything going on for you (your agents at work, your music, your timers, your Mac) so you don't have to.

Hover the notch for music (with a visualizer Oyl surfs across as the song plays, which you can drag Oyl along to scrub, lyrics that stay on from song to song until you turn them off (in Midnight they float in the sky under the open notch, whatever page is up, the sung line's words lighting like stars as the last line dissolves into stardust; the phone remote shows them too; and in Midnight a setting keeps them up while the notch is closed, right in the menu bar's strip beside it, on no ground of their own: each line fades in faintly, then sparkles in word by word on the other side of the notch from the last (a break between them aside, so they're sung either side about as much), the first on the left, as the last fades slowly away, a long one stretching most of the way along its side before it shrinks; a break in a song, and a song without lyrics all through, is a constellation either side of the notch, flaring and rippling to the beat; from song to song, and as the music pauses and plays, they fade quickly out and in; and never in the way of a click), cover-art controls (always the song's own cover: Spotify shows a song switched to its music video with a still from the video, so Oyl looks up the song's cover instead, the video playing on in Spotify), and the song's name, which a click takes you to Spotify or Music from; a song Spotify's playing on another device, like your phone, is followed as the Mac's Spotify tells of each change (a song starting, a pause, a skip, a seek), so its lyrics keep time, though its scripting can go on saying an old song's playing here; with nothing told yet, a song that isn't heard on the Mac counts as paused), timers and reminders (a name can be as long as you like, wrapping onto as many lines as it takes, and ⌘C, ⌘V, ⌘X, ⌘A and ⌘Z work as they do anywhere; click one to change its time or name, or + to type how much more it gets, which lengthens what it was set for as well as what's left; one that's gone off can have one or five more; pin one to hold it on the closed notch, its ring and name left of the notch and its time right, in either look, and from the phone too), an Amphetamine-style keep-awake (with the lid closed and no other screen on, it turns the screen all the way down and off, and Oyl stops drawing, analyzing the music and polling the player till the lid opens), the Oyls page (where timers that went off in the past day rest, a click from running again), an activity monitor that also counts Claude Code tokens, and a mirror (the camera, for a look at yourself before a call; its tab can be turned off on the Oyls tab). Out on the menu bar, an Oyl turns up for each thing going on (and when it's over, waves goodbye where it stands, springs up twice and is gone in a puff of smoke, sparks of its color flying out of it; one whose work is done jumps for joy first; in Midnight a moyl bows, bobs twice and flares, scattering into stardust. No two ever walk along together looking like one: one sets off a moment after another, a little quicker or slower, or somewhere else): each Claude Code agent at work (in a pastel with an orange hat; in a terminal, an editor or the Claude app's Code tab), each of the Claude app's Cowork tasks while it works and for a quarter of an hour after (read off the transcript each keeps on the Mac, whether or not it's the one on screen, in any of the app's accounts), the chat showing in the Claude app, Muse (Meta's agent, open at muse.ai in Chrome or Safari, in a black hat: working while its page offers to stop and what it says is changing (Muse leaves its Stop up once it's done, so a page that's sat still a minute is done), and wanting you while it asks you to approve something, then gone once it's answered; a page that's offered to stop for over five minutes is at a task of its own in the background, so doesn't count; read while its tab's the one showing, which needs Oyl allowed to control the browser and in Accessibility; click it to bring its tab up), every other agent on the radar (below), an app taking notes (Granola in a meeting: a sage Oyl with a notepad, scribbling, now and then looking up at what's being said, for as long as it has the microphone, which macOS tells of any app without asking; in Midnight, a banded world ruled like paper, a little moon going busily round it for the pen), the song (in headphones, dragging its cover and a little visualizer along behind it, bopping its head to the beat, which Oyl finds in the music as it plays, and singing along once a pinky's shown the lyrics; for an audiobook, sat reading it instead, looking up now and then to think it over, its cover in a thought cloud), every timer and reminder (its countdown beside it, or with Countdowns off on the Oyls tab, only in its last three minutes), staying awake, a low battery, a late night, a fresh selfie. Right of the notch sits a calendar bar, with today's events along it (the Oyls tab can turn it off).

Oyl watches for agents universally, on any Mac, with its agent radar: a list of the agents it knows and how each gives itself away, since there's no one sign on a Mac that an AI is at work. Claude Code by its hooks (or, where they aren't set up, as a program); the Claude app's chats by its window (even from another Space: in a fullscreen one of its own, its window's still read, and its prompts to use a tool still come up for your OK; and a task waiting on you that isn't the one showing, as its sidebar says ("Awaiting …", "Needs …"), gets a card too, whose Allow or Deny opens the task and answers its prompt once it's up, or leaves it open in front if what it wants is something else), and Cowork by the transcripts its tasks keep. Agents and chats in a browser tab by their pages, which nearly all offer to stop while they work and ask you to approve or allow what they want to do: Muse, ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini, Perplexity, Grok, Copilot, DeepSeek, Le Chat, Manus, Lovable, v0, Bolt and Replit. Apps by their windows, the same way: the ChatGPT app, Codex and Perplexity. Programs run in a terminal or an editor by how busy they are (one at its prompt uses next to nothing; one at work, streaming its reply, a steady few percent of a core), each its own Oyl named for the folder it's working in, while it's at work and a while after: Codex, Gemini CLI, Aider, OpenCode, Goose, Amp, Cursor Agent, Crush, Qwen Code and Copilot CLI. And note takers by the microphone: Granola. Each agent's Oyl wears its own color of hat while it works (Claude's orange, ChatGPT's and Codex's green, Gemini's blue, Muse's black…). For anything else, bring up the app (or the site, in a browser) and right-click the notch: Watch it as an Agent, and it's on the list, watched by its windows (or its page) from then on; Stop Watching takes it off. And Oyl looks for agents it doesn't know: every half minute, which apps and programs hold a connection open to an AI service (OpenAI's, Anthropic's, Mistral's, Groq's, OpenRouter's, Deepgram's and the like), and one that keeps doing so is offered, once, at the foot of the Oyls page: "Notion seems to use AI. Watch it as an agent?" (No, and it's never asked again.) Browsers aren't, since a browser is one app for all its tabs, nor editors built on VS Code, whose windows can't be read without switching on their screen-reader support. All of it is read on the Mac, from what macOS tells any app of its own user's processes; nothing is sent anywhere.

Every feature can be turned off. The settings tab, the gear last in the notch's row of tabs (or Settings… on the notch's right-click), is a page of them all, in sections: the notch's pages (each tab can go), lyrics, the visualizer (and with it, listening to the music), the song's name opening its player, haptics, Wispr Flow; each kind of Oyl (agents', the music's, timers', keep-awake's, the battery's, bedtime's, the selfie's, the one hanging out), the countdowns, dimming the bar, the calendar bar; each agent, the radar's and yours (yours with an × to take them off the list), answering Claude Code in the notch, and looking for new ones; hand gestures, and each gesture on its own; the control taps, the song keys and keeping out of Mission Control's way; the Dock, each shelf, and labeling screenshots; Midnight Oyl, and opening at login. Point at one to see what it does. In Midnight Oyl the page is engraved, its rows drifting in one after another; under them all, the motto: Oyl, own your life.

The shelf, in Oyl's Dock (below), keeps your latest screenshots and screen recordings as you take them, wherever screenshots are set to go. Its button (a viewfinder) sits first past the Dock's line, the screenshots themselves only on the shelf: click it and the shelf comes up over the bar, to drag them straight into a chat, an email or any other app. Each is labeled with what's in it, on the Mac (Vision reads its text, and Apple's on-device model puts that in a few words where Apple Intelligence is on, else the biggest line is used; nothing leaves the Mac), the label on its tile over when it was taken, and the file renamed to it as soon as it has one (if it still has the name macOS gave it), so it goes into a chat named for what it is; right-click one from before to rename it too. The Dock fades out of the way once one's dragged off it, so the app under it can take the drop, or drop it on an app in the Dock to open it there. Drag any file onto the shelf's button or the shelf itself (or use its +) to keep it there too; right-click the button to choose files, or to open the screenshots' folder. Hovering a tile shows two ways to be rid of it, apart: × top right takes it off the shelf (the file stays), the bin top left moves the file to the Trash. And low right, a way to convert it to another format, saved beside the original: pictures to PNG, JPEG, HEIC, TIFF, GIF, BMP or PDF; a PDF's pages to pictures or its text to Word, RTF, HTML or plain text; Markdown, Word, RTF, HTML and plain text to PDF and each other; movies to MP4, MOV, an animated GIF (the first 30 seconds) or just their sound; sound to M4A, WAV, AIFF or FLAC; JSON, CSV and property lists to each other; folders to a zip and zips back. Anything at all can be zipped. Right-click a tile to copy, open, AirDrop or show it, take it off the shelf (the file stays) or move it to the Trash. Next to it is a second shelf, Recently Saved (a tray), with whatever you've just saved, newest first: what's in Downloads (what comes by AirDrop, downloads from the web, attachments saved out of a text with Save Attachment, and what's saved since into a folder in it, two folders down, as an app exporting into a folder of its own there; a folder that comes in whole, unzipped say, is one tile, not each file in it), what's come from elsewhere onto the Desktop (an attachment dragged out of Messages, say), and photos and videos saved into Photos from Messages (Add to Photos Library, Save Photo) or another app, on the Mac or an iPhone, but not what a camera took or your own screenshots. Not what's still downloading, or the Mac's screenshots, which are on theirs. A photo's copied out of Photos into Oyl's own folder, so it drags and converts like any file (taking it off the shelf leaves it in Photos). It works just as the screenshots' shelf does, and the first time it's opened macOS asks to let Oyl see Downloads and Photos. Either shelf grows out of its own tile in the bar as it opens, on the notch's spring, and goes back into it; in Midnight Oyl, the Dock's sky grows on up round it instead, new stars drawing on in the new room, and the shelf comes in out of a blur on a pool of night with no edge to it. The first time the shelf's opened, macOS asks to let Oyl see the Desktop.

Oyl has its own Dock too, along the bottom of the screen in the notch's look (the open notch turned over, in black; in Midnight Oyl, the band turned over, the screen's whole width: it blooms up and out from the middle of the foot of the screen as it comes, quicker than the band does, as the band does from the notch, and wilts back into it as it goes, its edge flowing in slow swells and lobes and dissolving into the desktop, the star chart's hour lines fanning out from the middle, stars glinting, star dust drifting and filaments and drops of light spilling up out of it). It shows what the macOS Dock does, read from its settings at its icon size: Finder, the apps kept in it, any others open, its folders (but not the Trash). Each is redrawn in the look: its glyph lifted off its icon (what stands out from the tile's own colors), white on a pale tile in Oyl's, as Oyl's own buttons are, and in Midnight Oyl's traced in silver with no tile, glints twinkling along its lines that brighten and quicken when it's pointed at. Finder's is a magnifying glass, Chrome's Google's G, Canva's the C of its name, and Activity Monitor's just its pulse, without the grid behind it. An icon with no one glyph on it stays as it is. Like the notch, it comes up as the pointer reaches the bottom edge under it, covering the macOS Dock (which should be set to hide itself), and goes when the pointer leaves, though not at a slip: it has some room round it, and waits a moment before it goes, more of both with a shelf up (beside the bar under the shelf counts too), and a little longer just after a menu's closed somewhere off it. Click an app to open or switch to it, right-click to hide it, quit it or show it in Finder. ⌘-click one that's open for another window of it, as its own New Window makes one (an app without one gets a new instance of it instead); ⌥-click one to quit it, once you've said so over its icon (Quit, or Cancel; or click elsewhere, or move away). Finder can't be quit, so ⌥-clicking it just opens it. Drag one along the Dock to move it, the others making way (Finder stays first); the order is Oyl's own, and the macOS Dock's is left as it is. The shelf is in it (above). It comes up mid-drag too, as the Dock does: drop files on an app to open them in it. To turn the Dock off, untick Oyl's Dock on the Oyls tab (or right-click the notch). Right-click the notch to quit Oyl (it opens again at login, unless that's turned off in Settings), or Turn Off Oyl, which also keeps it from opening at login till you open it again; the Oyls tab has Turn off Oyl too. Either ends a keep-awake. Tap control twice on its own to take a screenshot of part of the screen, just as ⇧⌘4 does, half a second after (in case a third tap's coming): Oyl presses the Mac's own shortcut for it, whatever it's set to, so it's saved and shown as macOS's are; with that shortcut turned off, `screencapture` takes it, which needs Oyl allowed Screen Recording. Or tap control three times to switch Oyl off without quitting (no screenshot on the way): the notch blinks and everything's put away, the camera and microphone with it, till three more taps bring it back (or Oyl starts again). Switching off ends a keep-awake too (and the two-tap screenshot with it), and meanwhile Claude Code asks for its OKs the usual way; timers carry on, and still chime. The taps need Oyl allowed in Accessibility, as the hand pointer does. Control-Option-→ skips to the next song from any app, and Control-Option-← goes back (to the start of the song, or the one before if it's only just begun, as ⏮ does); nothing on the Mac uses them (VS Code only, to move by part of a word), and they're only taken while there's a song on and Oyl's on, so otherwise they go through as ever. They need Accessibility too. While you're viewing your desktops (Mission Control, App Exposé or Show Desktop), the notch, the Oyls and the Dock keep out of the way, none of them opening or taking a click till it's gone; that needs Accessibility too.

Control and Option tapped together turn the camera on quietly for 3 seconds, wherever the pointer is and whether the notch is open or not, so a gesture works from anywhere (nothing shows it but the Mac's camera light); tapped twice, it stays on till they're tapped twice again, or an OK's held up (with gestures off, they do nothing). With the camera on, hand gestures over the open notch play, pause and skip, turn the Mac's volume (pinch and twist; add the pinky to scrub), take a selfie (✌️; it's saved in ~/Pictures/Oyl side by side with a screenshot of your screen, once Oyl's allowed Screen Recording, though its Oyl holds up just you), keep the Mac awake (🤟), and show the lyrics (pinky out, thumb tucked in). Holding the ring finger down with the thumb, the other three fingers up, makes every Oyl say what it's doing; tipped over to the left, that hand sends them dashing left to line up, and to the right, flings them off to the right. The camera tracks at 640×480 (full size only for a selfie) and reads fewer frames when the Mac is busy or hot. Center Stage is off for Oyl alone (other apps keep it as you have it), so the camera sees all it can and holds still: where your hand is in the picture always means the same place.

With [Wispr Flow](https://wisprflow.ai) installed, the notch shows when it's listening, however you started it: a microphone on one side of the closed notch and your voice on the other (with microphone access, which Oyl only uses then). The microphone button at the top right of the open notch starts and stops its hands-free dictation, through links Wispr Flow handles but doesn't document, so a later version could break it.

The ideas page (the lightbulb tab) is a place to dump ideas to come back to, kept in tod's brain dump so they're in tod too. Type one and press Return; the field stays ready for the next. Each idea is one of three kinds, read from the same line. A deadline (“essay due fri”, or just a day, “lab report thu 5pm”) can't slip: it's always in sight under Due, soonest first, and says when it's late. An aim (“plan the trip in a month or so”, “by next week”, “early dec”, “by fri”, “dentist next week-ish”) is a day to have it done by, roughly, and can slip: it waits under Later, said as loosely as it was meant (“mid Dec”), moves up to Coming up in its last week, and once it's past, says how long it's slipped. Anything else is for Someday, and a month after it was jotted (or last kept) it asks “still want it?”. Point at an idea to put it off a week or a month, or keep it another month; check it off when it's done. Deadlines and aims are kept apart so the aims don't make the deadlines easy to ignore (as Amazing Marvin's due and end dates do, or Todoist's deadlines and dates), aims come back when their time comes as a tickler file's do, and the undated are looked over monthly, as a bullet journal's are. The phone remote has it too, to jot ideas on the go.

Oyl's phone remote puts the notch on your phone, from its browser, over the same Wi-Fi, or from anywhere with [Tailscale](https://tailscale.com) running on both. Turn it on in Settings, under Oyl, then choose Pair a phone: the notch shows a QR code for its camera (or six digits to type at the address beside it), good for five minutes and five tries. Paired, the phone keeps a long random key as a cookie (Oyl keeps only its hash), and the page shows what's waiting for you, with Allow and Deny for Claude Code's requests (while it's waiting on Oyl, about 25 seconds); your agents and what each is doing; the song, with its cover and controls; timers, to start, pause and stop; and keeping the Mac awake. It's in whichever look Oyl is in: Oyl's own, black and rounded like the notch, or Midnight Oyl's, its sky turning slowly round a pole off to one side, engraved rules in place of boxes, each part drifting in. Add it to the Home Screen and it opens like an app. It only answers devices on the Mac's own Wi-Fi or Ethernet (not a VPN, or the internet), sent to the Mac by name, and anything that changes something has to come from its own page. Forget phones, beside Pair, unpairs them all; turned off, nothing's listening. It's plain HTTP, so on its own it's for your own Wi-Fi rather than a shared one. Campus and café networks often keep devices from reaching each other at all. For those, run Tailscale on the Mac and the phone (signed in to the same account). Its tunnel is encrypted and works over any network, a phone's own data too. While it's running, the pairing code and the address go by the Mac's name on your tailnet, so one pairing works at school and at home alike. The remote also answers your devices on the tailnet, but only ones that come in on the Mac's own tailnet address, never a device on the Wi-Fi with an address that looks like one.

Midnight Oyl (right-click the notch, or on the Oyls tab) is a second look, made more to be looked at than used: a Victorian celestial atlas plate seen through TouchDesigner's glass. Opening the notch (gently, easing to rest with no bounce) runs a line of night out along the menu bar, then lets it fall into a band across the whole top of the screen, the sky engraved on it in silver-white: a star chart round the notch as its pole, stars that light up outward as it opens, a planisphere and an orrery that draw on and turn, a moon that tells the time (new at midnight, it waxes through the morning, lit by as much of its face as the morning's gone, till it's full at noon; then it wanes, dark by as much as the afternoon and evening have, and round it a dial of the day, midnight at its top, with a glint for now), constellations, drifting star dust and the odd shooting star. It has no edge: its foot flows in slow swells and lobes and dissolves into the desktop, with motes, stars, filaments and drops of light spilling out past it. It reaches down only as far as the page needs, a little sky under it. Every word on the pages is set in a High Victorian face, and their colors are paled toward white. With gestures on, the camera's on while it's open but never shown (with them off, it stays off, and the rock sign isn't looked for): your hands are, as constellations in its sky, each joint a star, named for the gesture they're making, so there's no separate camera tab. Click the moon to keep it open as a display, and again to let it close. It stays open only while the pointer's over its page (as big as the open notch would be, and a little round it) or on the way over to the moon, not anywhere in its sky, so it's as easy to leave as the notch. The Oyls are moyls: small, real-looking planets in their Oyls' colors, lit from one side with a thin rim of air, each a world that fits its job (an agent's a banded gas giant with a storm and, while Claude works, an orange polar cap; the song's a ringed one, its rings swelling on the beat (an audiobook's, its rings still, a little moon going slowly round as its glow comes and goes); a timer's an ice giant, its moon going round once over its time; staying awake's an ocean world under cloud; the battery's and bedtime's cratered rock), with a flare for a cheer. They're never on the menu bar: when the band opens they swoop down into its sky and drift among the stars, there to be clicked, and are gone again as it closes; when they announce what they're doing, each wears a ring of engraved capitals all the way round it saying so. On the music page, the wave is a galaxy in the cover's colors, its stars twinkling where the wave rises over them, and riding its crest as the scrubber is a bright four-pointed star. Holding up the rock 'n' roll sign (thumb and little finger out) turns your hands into the pointer, manus mode: each hand is a pointer of its own, a small constellation of it at its index fingertip (its stars born as it starts, out from the wrist, each a faint cloud gathering in and lit in a flash; and as it stops, each flaring and bursting, faintly, in a few sparks), and the cursor (hidden meanwhile, until the trackpad moves it) goes with whichever hand is doing something. Pinching your index finger and thumb clicks (holding the pinch holds, to drag; two quick pinches double-click), and so does resting the pointer on something a moment, a ring of dots drawing round it till it clicks. Touching your thumb to your middle fingertip instead, the index still pointing, right-clicks. Two fingers held together, side by side, are two on the trackpad: move them to scroll. Three together swipe as the trackpad's set to: across between Spaces and full-screen apps, up for Mission Control, down for the app's windows. A flick of the wrist works too, at a wrist's pace (a turn away from how the fingers pointed as they came together needn't be as quick as a dart of the whole hand, and a camera slowed to 15 frames a second in dim light reads it as well): with two fingers up it scrolls the way they flicked, as far as their tips swept, the page gliding on as a trackpad's does once a flicking finger lifts (even if the hand stops dead with its fingers still together, or the camera loses them mid-flick); with three it swipes, left or right between Spaces. Bringing the hand back doesn't scroll or swipe back, and flicking again the same way goes on. To go the other way, hold it still a moment first; with two fingers, that also stops the glide, and moving slowly scrolls as before. Curling the fingers in to let go isn't a flick. Both hands pinching zoom the app in front: draw them apart to zoom in, together to zoom out. It says what it's doing in signs rather than words: Gemini's ♊︎ while scrolling, a triangle while swiping, ⊕ and ⊖ as it zooms, a padlock if it can't click. To put it down, make the sign again, or rest the pointer a moment on the little sundial in the top bar, left of the band's page. It needs Accessibility, for the clicks, scrolls, swipes and zooms; without it the pointer moves but can't click. Gestures have to be on.

## Building

Needs macOS 14.2 or later and the Xcode Command Line Tools. No Xcode project.

```sh
./build.sh          # builds build/Oyl.app
./build.sh --run    # builds, then (re)launches it
```

The first build makes a local signing certificate in its own keychain, so macOS keeps the app's permissions across rebuilds.

The app icon (just Oyl, on a dark tile) is drawn with the app's own Oyl: `Icon/make.sh` redraws it from `Icon/Icon.swift` into `Icon/Oyl.icns`, which the build puts in the app.

## Claude Code

Oyl follows Claude Code sessions through hooks, which it installs as scripts in `~/Library/Application Support/nott/` each time it starts. Point Claude Code's hooks at `agent-hook` for its events, and at `permission-hook` for `PermissionRequest` to answer approvals from Oyl. A request for your OK, Claude Code's or the Claude app's, comes up as a small card in the screen's bottom right corner rather than opening the notch: who's asking, what it wants (and the command, file or site), Deny and Allow (or a thumbs up while the camera's looking), and a click on the rest to answer it in the app instead; with more waiting, how many. In Oyl's look it's a black card with the asking Oyl on it; in Midnight's, a patch of night with no edge, a star glinting for the agent among a few twinkling specks, coming up out of a blur. It's there on every Space, over fullscreen apps too. A session's Oyl goes when it ends or its process quits, and once it's been idle ten minutes (finished in an editor's panel or a terminal's tab left open), coming back as soon as it's at work again; so does every agent's but Cowork's tasks, which go a quarter of an hour after they last did anything. Stopped with Esc, Claude Code doesn't say so through its hooks, so Oyl reads it off the end of the session's transcript and lets its Oyl rest.

The calendar bar reads the Mac's calendars, in each calendar's own color, so an account's calendars show up once it's added in System Settings › Internet Accounts. Clicking the bar opens Calendar.
